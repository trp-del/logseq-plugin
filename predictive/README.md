# Predictive

One module of Logseq Plugin (the root README says how the six fit together; the grammar is in the workspace's value-craft skill, `references/grammar.md`).

The structure of your graph used while you write, without making you fill in fields.

- **Proposals.** You write a plain bullet. If it has a tag, the tag's properties show faintly in Logseq's own property pills under it, with values already in them. A page you linked fills the property that takes it, unless your graph shows otherwise: where four or more earlier notes with the tag link such a page and either hold it in this property or show it belongs elsewhere (the property holds another page, or the linked page sits in another property), the property gets it at once only if it held the page in nine of ten of them, as a guess from half, and not at all under half (the page then goes to the next property that takes it, so a doctor linked in a procedure goes to Performed by, not to a Referred by you seldom fill). The same holds for a page named in plain words. A date you wrote in words fills a date: "on Friday", "next Friday", "in two weeks", "the day after tomorrow", "a week from Monday", "next month", "end of the month", "early December", "mid-January", "the first Monday of December", "in six months", "three weeks ago", "tonight", "Christmas Eve", "Nov 20", "the 14th", and 20/11 or 11/20/2026 where only one reading is possible (a number over 12 is the day; 5/6 is left alone). Off a journal page the words say the day for any tag; on a journal page a tag whose nodes are dated the day they were written keeps that day (8 November 2026). A weekday in words that tell of what has been ("on Friday were normal") fills nothing: it could be the day gone or the day coming. When the bullet reads like earlier ones, the value they had is offered. Leave a bullet you wrote in and the sure ones are written; leave with Enter and the dotted guesses are written too. Going back into a bullet shows nothing until you change its words, so a suggestion you passed over does not come back to a bullet that has not changed; a bullet you only passed through is left as it was. A date with no year that went by in the last eight weeks is that day, further back it is next year's, and the pencil writes the day as Logseq will once it is filled in, so the value does not change when it is written: a day with a journal page by its page's title (Logseq keeps a day's name when the date format changes), a new day in the date format of Settings, Editor ("Nov 3rd, 2026", or "2026-11-03" with yyyy-MM-dd), and a change of that format is followed at the next pause in typing; your own weekday with no word like "next" is not read as a date. A bullet with no tag is offered none by itself (24 November 2026; until then a `#Tag` was pencilled at the right end of its line): its tags are on Option-T, below.
- **Basic meanings.** A sum of money in the words ("$84", "€12.50", "40 dollars", "$4,000.98", "€1.240,50", "$3.9k") goes to the number property your earlier bullets like this one put their sums in: with Amount and Payment on one tag, "Paid the gardener $35" fills Payment when earlier "Paid the …" bullets did, and "Gas bill came in at $61" fills Amount when earlier bills did. With no such bullets yet, it fills the one number property named for money (Amount, Cost, Price, Fee, Paid, Total and the like), and with two such names it says nothing, unless all but one are named for a part of the bill (a Fee, a Tip: see "The part each thing plays"). Each day in the words goes to the date its words say (17 November 2026, the owner: "recognize using words as cues for deadline vs scheduled vs start and end date vs completed"), a date's kind read from its name (Due date, Deadline: deadline; Scheduled, Appointment: scheduled; Start, Begin: start; End, Until, Expires: end; Completed, Done, Paid, Closed: completed). In this order, each day to one date only: a span ("from Jan 5 to Jun 30", "Jan 5–Jun 30", "between Dec 1 and Dec 12") is the start and the end; a date's own name right before a day takes it ("renews Mar 3", "due date Dec 5"); a word of its kind right before the day ("due", "by", "scheduled for", "booked", "coming", "starts", "since", "until", "ends", "expires"); for completed, a part of the sentence that says it was done, by a word ("paid", "fixed", "done", "filed") or any verb in the past for a day gone by ("Window replaced Nov 9, invoice due Dec 9" gives Completed Nov 9 and Due Dec 9). Two days with nothing to tell them apart fill nothing. On 28 sentences it was built with, 27 went whole to the right dates. On 25 new sentences written after it was finished and not used to change it, 20 went whole to the right dates, 4 missed a day ("before Thanksgiving", "from tomorrow", "come the 3rd") and 1 gave a day it should not ("the Nov 20 storm may delay things" pencilled as Scheduled, since a day ahead with no word is taken as the scheduled one); no day went to a date of the wrong kind. On 408 more sentences built from wordings it had never been given and run once, 244 came out whole, 137 missed a day ("cutoff Dec 4", "visit Dec 4", "commences Dec 4", "last day Dec 4"), 9 filled a day that was no date of the job ("receipt dated Oct 28", "weather on Friday looks bad"), and none put a day in a date of the wrong kind: what it does not know it leaves empty. It learns the wordings it does not know from your graph (18 November 2026, `core/date-words.js`): every node where you filled a date by hand teaches that the words right before its day ("cutoff" in "Gutters cutoff Dec 4", "last day" in "Hedge trim last day Dec 22") go to that date, so the next "Garage roof cutoff Dec 10" pencils Due date Dec 10, with "You put a day after these words in Due date before" on hover. One node is enough; a day you moved to another date teaches the same way once the node is written, and taught twice it goes before the word lists. Words are matched in their base forms ("commenced" teaches "commences"), a day ahead and a day gone by are taught apart ("visit Dec 4" Scheduled, "visited Nov 9" Completed), a day in the words you put in no date counts against its words, and small words ("up", "than", "me") are never taught alone. Measured by teaching each wording once, on another subject and another day: the 408 sentences went from 267 whole to 321, and a further 408 written and run once as a frozen set went from 244 to 338 (355 after three reading mistakes it showed were fixed: "the day before yesterday" was read as a deadline, "ends by Friday" as a deadline, "Monday until Wednesday" ended a year on), with no day in a date of the wrong kind. Apple's on-Mac word tool was tried for words close in meaning and not used: it puts opposite words together ("concludes", "closes" and "stops" read as start words) and was right on 20 of 47 words. A full stop after a short month ("Dec. 4") no longer ends the sentence. A day with no word at all is left alone, except a day ahead said as an appointment ("on Monday", "Dec 4 at 10am"), which is the scheduled one; guessing every such day put 49 of 456 in the wrong place. "Next Monday" on a Tuesday is read as the Monday a week after the coming one, as "next" is read everywhere here. A day right after "due", "deadline" or "by" ("due Friday", "due by the 4th", "renew by Nov 20") fills the date named for a deadline, Logseq's own Deadline included, and is then no other date's; a day after "scheduled for", "booked for" or "planned for" fills Scheduled or a date named so. "Due to the pain" is a cause, and "diagnosed by Dr. Chen on Friday" is no deadline, since the day must follow the word. A sum after a number property's name is that property's, read whole with its commas: "premium $1,240 coverage $350,000 deductible $1,000" gives Premium 1240, Coverage 350000 and Deductible 1000, and a sum right after one name is never read as standing before the next (17 November 2026: it had given Coverage 240 and Deductible 0). Of several sums, the one after "total", "came to" or "=", or right before "total", is the sum ("$84 and wine $22, $106 total"); several with nothing to tell them apart ("Dinner $120 and drinks $40") fill nothing; a sum the words set aside ("Dinner $120, Sam paid back $60") leaves the other as the sum. Where the tag takes a sum and the words hold none with its currency, one plain number is the sum ("Gas 64.28", "Paycheck 3850"), unless it is part of a day ("Nov 20", "the 4th"), a time ("8:30", "8am"), a count of things ("2 lattes", "x2") or a year, or follows a word that makes it something else ("for 2", "cycle 5"). A sum in the words never stops words written before from naming their tag in Option-T's list: "Gas 64.28" lists the tag "Gas" had first. On the Personal Finance graph, with each bullet's real sum written into its words, every way of writing it filled Amount right on 107 to 109 of 109 bullets, and none wrong (17 November 2026). Each is a dotted guess, written by Enter or a click. The words are the locale's (`core/dates.js`), so another language adds its own.
- **The part each thing plays.** Which property a value belongs in is read from the sentence, not from the order it is written in (22 November 2026, the owner: "I want the property filling to be very sophisticated and have very nuanced way of distinguishing language"; `core/roles.js`, its words in `core/dates.js`). **A page** named in the words, linked or by its whole name, is read three ways. Set aside, it fills nothing: denied ("Did not see [[Dr. Chen]]", "Coffee at [[Blue Bottle]], not [[Starbucks]]"), replaced ("instead of", "rather than", "in place of"), left out ("without"), compared with ("cheaper than [[Target]]"), spoken about ("about [[Dr. Patel]]'s report", any possessive, "what Dr. Chen meant"), in a clause about another ("who trained under"), or absent ("was away", "is on leave"). Given a part, it goes to the property named for that part and to no other: the small word before it (from, out of, to, into, by, with, for), an arrow ("A → B"), the verb it is the subject or the object of ("Sam lent Ana", "ordered by", "Ben to send", "Topped up Ally Savings"). A property's own name says the part it takes: "From account" and "To account" a side, "Referred by" and "Performed by" who did that, "Bought for" the one it was for, "Lender" and "Organizer" who does it, "Assignee" who it is done to; verbs that tell one event from two sides are joined (lend and borrow and owe, pay and charge, send and receive), so "Borrowed $40 from Sam" makes Sam the Lender. And a property named for one part leaves a page the words give another ("Lunch with Sam" is not Bought for; "Email Ana the minutes" is not her task), unless your graph shows the property takes nearly every such page. A page the words say nothing of goes where it went before, written at once only where no other one-value property could as well take it; otherwise a guess. Several pages for one place pass to a later property that holds several ("Met Ben and Chloe"). A page the words gave one property is never offered for another from look-alike bullets. **A sum** named as a part of the bill ("$15 tip", "tipped $8", "fee of $3", "a $5 coupon", "$20 off") is that part's property where the tag has one, and never the price; a sum compared with ("$7 less than"), replaced ("was $200, paid $150", "from $80 to $95", "not $30"), the whole a part was paid of ("$50 of the $95 bill") or multiplied ("2 x $35 = $70") is set aside; what is left is the price, so "Dinner $84 plus $15 tip" fills Amount 84 where it filled nothing. Two prices with nothing to tell them apart still fill nothing. **A day** set aside for another is taken out before the days are read: "moved from Dec 5 to Dec 12" and "was due Nov 20, pushed to Nov 27" give the later day to the date the first was for, "by Dec 4, not Dec 1" the first, "originally due Dec 1, now due Dec 8" the second, "no longer due Dec 5" none. A date named for a day to come (Due, Deadline, End, Scheduled, or a name ending in "by": "Repay by") is no longer guessed as the day of writing. Measured on sentences written by hand for this, where only the words say where a value goes (`node tools/nuance/measure.js`, a pretend graph with no earlier lines; about no one real): on the 81 it was built with, 11 came out whole before and 27 put a value where it did not belong, now 81 and none; on 70 more written after it was finished and first run once (`--fresh`), 62 whole, 4 missed and 4 wrong (before: 12, 33 and 25), and 70 after the eight were fixed. Both sets are now tests. They are my sentences on a pretend graph: they show the reading works, not how right it is on your notes.
- **Lessons.** Predictive learns from how you answer its suggestions, separately in each graph, and keeps what it learned on a page of that graph, **Predictive lessons**, one plain line per lesson ("earlier lines · Provider: taken 1, said no 5"; "your words · Amount: moved to Reimbursed 2"). A lesson is one source (earlier lines, your words, a linked page, the day written) and one property, or a tag guessed for a note from the Alfred workflow. Taken is Enter or a click; said no is the ×, the faded arrow, or another value you set by hand instead; moved is the same sum or day set by hand in another property of the same kind. A suggestion you only pass over teaches nothing. Said no at least three times and more than twice as often as taken, that source stops suggesting that value; moved to the same property twice and more often than taken, the value is suggested there, and its hover says so. A sure value (a page you linked) is never held back. Delete a line on the page to make Predictive forget that lesson, or the page to forget them all. Only names of tags and properties and counts are written there, never your words, and the page's lines are never learned from as notes. Lines learned from Apple's tools before 24 November 2026 stay on the page as they are; nothing reads them.
- **Suggested links.** As you type, the rest of a page's name shows in grey after the cursor, as on a phone. Right arrow, or a tap of the Hyper key (Caps Lock as Cmd+Ctrl+Opt+Shift), turns the words into `[[the page]]`. Anything else ignores it. Escape takes the grey words alone: the next Escape hides the proposals, the one after leaves the bullet. Inside `[[ ]]`, while Logseq's own list is open under the brackets, a name that is nearly a page's name shows that page after the words with an arrow ("[[Dr Chen" shows "→ Dr. Mei Chen", "[[Cisplatine" shows "→ Cisplatin"), so a second page is not made for the same thing: the words of the name out of order or with gaps, without its punctuation, or one letter off a one-word name. Right arrow writes the page's name in the brackets. A name typed whole, or from its start, is Logseq's list's own.
- **Your capitals in a link.** Logseq shows a [[link]] by the page's own name, so [[dr. mei chen]] would read Dr. Mei Chen. When the name you typed differs from the page's only in its capitals, typed whole or picked from Logseq's list after typing it whole, the link keeps your words when you leave the bullet: it is written as Logseq's link with its own words, [dr. mei chen]([[Dr. Mei Chen]]), which looks and opens as any link. When you go back into the bullet it shows as you typed it, [[dr. mei chen]], and is written back the same way when you leave. A few letters and then a page picked from Logseq's list is left as Logseq wrote it. A suggested link taken with Right arrow or the Hyper key is written in the capitals you were typing in: dr. mei, taken, gives dr. mei chen, and the grey words show it so. Cmd-Z puts the page's name back. A setting (Keep my capitals in a link I type) turns it off.
- **Two ways, and Option-P.** The setting **When proposals show** has two ways (22 November 2026, the owner: "either it is left on, or I turn it on per bullet"). *As I write* (the default): proposals come by themselves once typing pauses (200 ms), as above. *When I press the key*: nothing is worked out, shown or filled in on a bullet, sure values included, until **Option-P** is pressed in it; from then until the bullet is left, or Escape lets them go, they follow its words, and typing after Escape does not bring them back. In either way Option-P shows them at once, without the pause, also on a bullet you have not changed, and what it shows is filled in as shown when you leave. Enter takes them all and goes on to the next bullet; Escape lets them go. When there is nothing to propose, "Nothing to suggest" shows faintly right after the bullet's words for two seconds (or until the next key), so a key that found nothing is not taken for a key that did nothing. Measured in a test Logseq: about 25 ms from Option-P to the pencil on a bullet whose tag is already drawn (working out the values takes under 10 ms of it); as you write, about 200 ms after the last key, the pause, so nothing is worked out between two letters. The key is a setting (Keys for showing the proposals now); Option-P is one Logseq does not use.
- **Option-T** on a bullet with no tag lists the tags it could be, best first, to pick from (Up and Down, Enter, or a click; Escape closes; typing on, a digit included, closes it too). The tags are those that have a property taking the pages the line names, linked or in plain words (a doctor can be the Provider of a Consultation, who you Spoke with in a Conversation, who Decided a Decision); a property that takes pages of any tag (a Topic) is no reason, since almost any page fits it (14 November 2026: a note about a family was offered as a Consultation, a reason for a decision as a Conversation). Each row says its reason, the property that would take the pages named ("Spoke with: Ruth Okoro, Amara Okoro"; "Symptom type: Tingling fingers"); a tag read from the words says so, with the properties it brings; the tag the same words carried before comes first ("written like this before"). They are put in order by the words of the earlier lines nearest this one; a line that names nothing is ordered by its words alone. When the tags listed sit under more than one of your top-level tags, each group stands under that tag's name (Occurrence over Consultation and Conversation, Phenomena over Cancer and Disease), so the list shows where each guess sits; the best guess stays first, the groups follow in the order their best guess came, and the keys pass over the names (14 November 2026). Picking gives the bullet the tag and its properties are proposed as for any tagged bullet, with the faded arrow to put the tag back. Nothing is written until you pick.
- **Option-C**, or **/Cite**, says where a bullet came from, at the cursor. A line rolls out under the bullet and typing finds among the pages and the PDFs (Up and Down, Enter, or a click; a name that starts with the words comes first, a PDF before a page). Last in the list stands what you typed: a web address as a link, anything else as plain words, for a source that is not in the graph. A PDF or plain words then ask for the page; Enter with none leaves the page out. The words get `(the source, p. 3)` where the /Cite was, the bullet is left, and a faded arrow takes the citation off. Escape goes back one step, and from the first step back to the words with nothing written. A press on a citation to a PDF opens the PDF at that page. A citation to a page or PDF is a link in the words, so the source lists the bullet in Threads and in Logseq's references. A web address is written as a web link, plain words in italics in brackets, so words in italics alone in brackets read as a citation too.
- **Cmd-;** in a bullet makes its words a page. The bullet stays in the outline, open, with the cursor in its first child, and its children are the page's: the same children in both places. With a page by that name already, the bullet shows that page, with the page's children under it. Either way the words read as a link: a press opens the page, Shift in the right sidebar, and the name is changed on the page. A faded arrow turns a new page back into a bullet. A bullet showing a page keeps its own words in the graph under the page shown, so nothing is lost if Logseq stops halfway, and its faded arrow takes the page off again and shows the words as they were. The key is a setting, and Cmd-P stays Logseq's list of commands.
- **One $.** Logseq pairs a typed $ for math, so "paid $5" was kept as "paid $5$". A $ typed with nothing selected now stays one $ (17 November 2026, the owner: "don't double type $ in logseq"); $$ for math is written by typing it twice, and a $ typed over selected words still wraps them as Logseq does. Each typed $ is followed up on its own, so a key pressed right after it cannot leave Logseq's second $ behind (22 November 2026: typed fast, "Dinner $84 plus $15 tip" kept a closing $, which then began the next bullet as "$Jacket …"). A bullet that still ends in such paired $s is read without them, so the day or sum before them is read. (`logseq/one-dollar.js`)
- **<<** typed in a bullet, at its start or after a space, opens Logseq's own Node embed search there, as /Node embed does: the marks go, typing finds a node or page, Enter shows it in the bullet, Cmd-Z takes it back. In a bullet with words, the embed goes in a new bullet under it, as Logseq's own does, and the words stay. Escape closes the search and gives back << with what was typed after it. Inside a word or in `code` it stays text. (( stays Logseq's block reference. The marks are a setting (Typing that embeds a node); empty switches it off.
- **Option-Enter** steps through a bullet's properties using Logseq's own lists: it opens the first empty or guessed one with the guess already typed in, and the property keeps its pencil while its list is open; Enter keeps it and moves on, Tab skips, Escape comes back to the words, and a date stop takes words like "next fri". On a bullet you came back to and have not changed, nothing is proposed, so the stops open empty. Down stays Logseq's: at the end of a tagged bullet it rests on the first property, and from there Tab, Left and Right step the same way.

None of this happens while a table's cell is being written (the Name of a row, opened over the table): that is a plain edit, as in Logseq without the plugin.

Proposals stay out of the way: they sit where the value will go, nothing is written until you leave the bullet, a guess you carry past is dropped, Escape hides them, a click on one accepts it, its × (under the pointer) takes it off and keeps it from being proposed on that bullet again, and a faded arrow after the bullet puts back what was filled in and keeps it from being proposed on that bullet again. A new fill's arrow takes the last one's place. What a × or an arrow took off stays off that bullet after Logseq restarts too: the bullet's id and the kind of value are kept on this Mac, never the words.

Proposals move nothing you did not move. Under a bullet that shows no row of properties yet, the row of proposed ones rolls open out of the bullet and closed into it, so the bullets under it slide rather than jump. A proposal whose value changes keeps its place, and the new words fade in over the old. A proposed page's name carries its sort's mark, as it will once written, so when you leave the bullet the pencils turn to the written values in place: nothing slides. The × takes no room either: it appears under the pointer over the gap after the value. Properties with no value stay hidden under a bullet until something is proposed for them, and show faint under the bullet you are writing (the one setting for this, **Hide empty properties until something is proposed**; with it off they stay on screen, faint). When every property under a bullet is hidden so, the row they stand in goes too, leaving no gap; and a folded bullet with nothing under it but such empty properties shows Logseq's plain dot rather than its folded ring, and such a bullet, folded or open, has no fold arrow under the pointer, since opening it would show nothing (11 November 2026, the owner: "bullets have this open view but empty so just have a gap"; the arrow: "why do chevrons appear on bullets that upon opening still show nothing?").

It is made from the two features of the author's logseq-db-refinements plugin that did this, rebuilt to run on any graph.

## What it writes

The Predictive lessons page and its lines, a moment after a lesson changes (see Lessons above). Property values on the bullet you are leaving, or on its parent bullet when only the parent's tags give the properties, and the tag you pick with Option-T. For a note from the Alfred workflow's Add panel, the note, the tag taken there and its values, with one Undo. A citation you pick, in the bullet's words. With Cmd-;, the Page tag on a bullet, or Logseq's Node embed in it, and an empty first child. A page you linked with a tag right after it in the words is given that tag when the value is filled in, and Undo takes it off again. A date that names a day with no journal page yet makes that journal page; Undo takes the value and leaves the page, empty. Option-Enter's stepping can add the bullet that comes next. Leaving a bullet whose words changed writes the sure values and leaves the guesses, by the owner's ruling of 31 October. A value fills only a property still empty as it is written: one you set by hand while the pencil showed is yours, and stays. With Cmd-; on a bullet that will show a page, one write makes the bullet show it (the link Logseq's own Node embed sets) and the bullet's words are never written. The lessons page is read before each save: if the read fails nothing is written and the save is tried again later, so a lesson is never written a second time; and when the page comes back out of the Recycle only the plugin's own lines are cleared from it, never a line you wrote there.

## Why it works on any graph

Nothing in the code names a tag, a property, a page or a word of any particular graph. What the original assumed, and what replaced it:

| The original assumed | Here |
|---|---|
| A date property is the one titled "date" | A date is any property of type date. Whether the journal day suits it is read from the graph: for each tag, how many of its nodes carry the day they were written on and how many another day (`dated`). |
| A fixed list of English words "name nothing" (today, morning, with …) | The graph's own lines say which words are too common to name a page (`ctx.common`), in any language. |
| Dates in words are English | A locale object holds the words (`core/dates.js`), the words of money, "due" and "scheduled for" too; English ships. |
| Limits tuned on one graph (nearest ≥ .3, lead .7, tag nearest ≥ .6) | Limits live in the context (`core/engine.js`). |
| One start date, one `Due`, … | With two open date properties (a start and an end) the words cannot say which, so only the graph's evidence speaks. Logseq's own Deadline and Scheduled are proposed only with evidence, never by the fallback. |
| Choices (Status …) | Never proposed. Where a thing stands is rarely in its words. |

A graph with no tags, no properties, or no earlier lines gets no proposals and no errors (tested). The plugin learns only from what the graph already holds: the more you write in a consistent way, the more it can offer.

## Settings

The settings are in the plugin's one settings window, under Predictive (**Plugin settings** in the command palette, or the gear on the plugin's card).

## The look is Look's

The pencilled values (regular italic, grey, the dotted line), the tags in the Option-T list and the Undo arrow's fade take their look from the shared class names on what Predictive draws (`lk-pencil`, `lk-tag`, `lk-prop`, `lk-undo`; the list is in Look's README), which stand whichever modules run. With Look switched off a pencil still reads as a pencil; Logseq's own property line around it goes back to its capsules.

## How it is built


Evidence, from most to least trusted: **sure** (a page linked in the words that fits a property; the page the bullet is on; the journal day where the graph always dates a thing the day it is written) and **guesses** (resemblance to earlier lines; plain names; dates and numbers in words; the parent's values). Where several rules speak they must agree or nothing is proposed. A wrong fill taken by habit is the one thing that must not happen; silence costs nothing.

The pretend graphs share nothing: an English work graph (Owner, Assignee, Project, **When**, **Due**), a Spanish reading log (Autor, **Género**, **Leído el**) and an empty graph. On them, sure values were never wrong and guesses were right 99% of the time or better. These graphs are tidy and repetitive, so read that as "the rules work and nothing is tied to one graph", not as the accuracy you will see on your own notes.

### The tag of a line, where one is asked for

No bullet is given a tag by itself any more (24 November 2026). The guess is still worked out in two places that ask for it: the Alfred workflow's Add panel, which shows one tag for the words typed there, taken with Enter (`logseq/outside-note.js`, `propose` with `offerTag`), and Relations, which puts the tag a mention's words look like first among the tags it offers for it (`tagFor`). Option-T does not use it: its list is `tagChoices` ("Choosing a tag with a key", below), which shares only the same words as before.

The guess is made in this order (`core/tag-score.js`), and only once the line has four words, unless its words were written before with a tag:

1. **The same words as before** (`twinOf`). Earlier lines whose words are just these words, each in its base form from the grammar library already in the plugin (compromise, `threads/vendor/compromise.js`: "Paid the bills" and "paid the bill" are one), give their tag when one tag holds four fifths of them; one such line is enough. Failing those, near twins: lines close in words (cosine .5) that name the same thing, the main noun ("insurance" in "car insurance paid", so "Phone case" is not taken for the Phone bill); two are needed. Untagged twins count against the tag only when they outnumber the tagged ones.
2. **The vote**: the nearest earlier lines vote for their tag, and the tag is offered when the nearest is near enough (`tagNearLeast` .3) and the leading tag far enough ahead (`tagLead` .7); plain notes vote "no tag".
3. **The word profiles** (`core/profile.js`, naive Bayes) with two **context clues** (whether the line is on a journal day, and the kind of each page its words link): the tag is offered when it leads the runner-up, plain lines included, by `tagGap` 5 in log-odds, and the near lines do not lean to another tag. Only tags that bullets carry have a profile, and only with ten bullet lines or more.

The three numbers are fixed in `logseq/app.js` at what the setting *How readily a tag is suggested* held at "balanced", its default; the setting went with the pencilled tag, since its row and its picture in the settings window were of a tag pencilled on a bullet. Someone who had set it to careful or eager now gets the balanced guess in the Add panel. Gone with the pencilled tag too: a shown tag held steady while the words went on (`steady`, `tagHold`), which only a bullet being typed needed.

What was measured while the tag was still pencilled on a bullet is in this file's earlier versions (the three settings on 45 hand-written lines, the same words as before on the Personal Finance test graphs, the context clues on 8,900 tagged bullets). The short of it: the vote alone found a tag for a fresh line seldom and was almost never wrong; with the profiles about one fresh line in two; the same words as before took the clean Personal Finance test graph from 10 right of 109 to 98, none wrong.

### Scoring the tag guess on a copy of a graph

`node tools/tag-score/score-tags.js <copy of db.sqlite>` reads a copy of a graph's file without Logseq (`read-graph.js`, read-only, a few seconds) and replays every tagged bullet a word at a time with its own line left out, for each way of guessing (`ways.js`): right, wrong and silent at the end, how often a shown guess was right, how many words the first right guess needed, how often the guess switched or vanished on the way, and how many plain lines got a tag. It reports three sets: all tagged bullets (the graph's look-alikes flatter it), fresh wording (no look-alike over .5), and the 59 hand-written lines of `test/graph/held-out.json`. `--sample N` takes every so many tagged bullets of a large graph. `clues.js` and `corrections.js` hold the ideas tried.

### Measuring on a large made-up graph

`node tools/harness/measure.js` measures Predictive outside Logseq, with the plugin's own core. The graph is made up (`tools/harness/corpus.js`): one fictional
patient, about no one real, 6,000 tagged notes and 1,500 plain ones that Predictive has learned, and 600 held-out notes with
150 plain ones that it is asked about. The held-out notes are written in sentences the learned notes never use, and where one
describes a symptom in other words, the words are new too, so a right answer is read from the note and not copied from a twin.
Each held-out note is asked as while writing, with its tag and no values. The report says what you would see (written at once,
pencilled, nothing) against what a careful person filled in, by what the value went on and by how the note carries the value (a
link, a name, part of a name, other words, not in the words); then the tag where one is asked for, and the plain notes tagged
anyway. `--set nearLeast=.4` (any limit) tries a setting before the plugin is changed; `--held`, `--notes`, `--plain` and `--seed`
change the graph, and `--untagged .5` leaves that share of its notes of each kind with no tag, as a person leaves many. It is
made up, so it shows how the parts behave and what a change does, not how right Predictive is on your own notes. On 24 November
2026, before and after the pieces above were taken out: 348 values written right and 9 wrong, 554 pencilled right and 32 wrong,
440 not shown, the same values note for note. (Until that day it also asked Apple's matcher and model through the helper.)

### Choosing a tag with a key

A tag given without asking would have to be right before it speaks, which on fresh writing is rarely. A key you press on purpose can be more generous, because you choose. Measured on the same 45 hand-written lines (`core/propose.js`, `tagChoices`; the numbers are of lines the graph has no twin of):

Hyper alone (Cmd, Control, Option and Shift, let go with no other key; setting **Hyper alone chooses a tag**) opens the same list on a bullet with no tag, the cursor kept where it was. One tap does one thing: when grey words of a page name show, it takes them and opens nothing (link-ui.js hears the tap and hands it to tag-pick.js only then).

| | lines | right tag first | right tag in the list |
|---|---|---|---|
| a page is named (linked or in plain words) | 18 | 15 | 16 |
| no page named: the words alone | 27 | 19 | 21 |
| all tagged lines | 45 | 34 (76%) | 37 (82%) |

A list was shown for 43 of the 45, and the lists are two to four tags long. On the 14 plain notes it would show a list for 13 if asked, and the order is then close to arbitrary: it is a menu, not a verdict, and the plugin never opens it by itself. A line that shares no word with anything the graph holds gets no list.

What the pages named add is the shortlist and little else. They cut the graph's 22 tags to two to four, and the true tag was among them nearly every time, but they never single out one tag, because a visit and a call take the same people; the words do the ordering. Tried as a way to guess more tags automatically (the vote restricted to those tags), the structure made things worse: it spoke more, but 12 of the extra tags were wrong on the graph's own lines, and it changed nothing on the 7 fresh lines that had a link. As a list to pick from it has no such cost.

### A bigger test graph

`test/graph/flesh-out.py` writes `flesh-out.json`: about 660 more blocks and 50 pages for the Illness Test Graph of the original plugin (a fictional patient; nothing in it is real), in that graph's own voice and conventions. It adds the months before the diagnosis, the days between and around the chemo cycles, a wider cast (people, doctors, symptoms, medicines, places, reports, decisions) and many more visits, calls and things coming up, with a few records half-filled on purpose. Then, with the Illness Test Graph open in Logseq and the plugin loaded:

```
python3 test/graph/flesh-out.py
node tools/graph/write-in-app.js --trial   # three pages, two days, to see it work
node tools/graph/write-in-app.js           # all of it
```

It writes through the plugin's own editor calls, so it merges into journal days that exist. It only adds, every block has a fixed id so a second run adds nothing, and it refuses any graph but the Illness Test Graph. (Logseq's command-line `graph import --type edn` into an existing graph reported success and added nothing here, which is why it is not used.) Back up the graph's folder first.

## What is NOT checked

Read this before trusting it with a graph you care about.

- **The taking out of 24 November 2026 has not been tried in Logseq by the one who made it.** The core's tests pass and every file loads against a pretend Logseq; what a bullet shows while you write, the settings window and the Alfred panel's guess were not looked at.
- **One Logseq version, one test graph.** Everything above ran on Logseq 2.0.2 and the Seed Check graph. The drawing and the stepping depend on Logseq's own page structure (`.bottom-property-pill`, `.cp__select`, the date picker's "e.g." box), which can change in any release. If Logseq changes it, proposals go quiet rather than wrong: a proposal that cannot be drawn is not written.
- **It writes to your notes.** Sure values are filled in when you leave a bullet, without asking, as in the original. Try it first in a copy of a graph, or set **Not in these graphs** to keep it out of the ones you care about.
- **Not checked in the app:** a bullet with the tag typed as `#Tag` (the tag's pill appears only after Logseq saves it), property rows shown as rows of their own (Notes) and not capsules, Left/Right stepping between capsules, clicking one name of several, a graph that is not in English, and large graphs.
- **Timing is measured on pretend data.** On 20,000 lines one proposal took about 8 ms here (it runs once typing pauses for 200 ms, not on every key; a bullet left within the pause still gets what was shown). The full reading of the graph is done in the background every five minutes, and with it how often each page's name is linked and written plain, a slice of lines at a time; a changed line then changes only its own count. What you took or passed over among suggested links is saved on leaving a bullet, at most once a minute.
- **The limits are only as good as your history.** A graph of a few dozen tagged lines has little to learn from. It also learns from what you write while testing, as it should: lines of test text change what it proposes next.
- **English only for dates in words**, until a locale is added. A day told by another event ("two days before the scan") is not read.
- **Properties that are text, and choices:** only link properties, dates and numbers are proposed. Text and choice properties are never filled.

## What was left out of the original

Proposals on a page's own properties (the original's `shadow-page.js`), `[[Page]]#Tag` giving the page the tag, the hidden-bracket font (a link just taken shows underlined without its brackets), picker ordering (the likely values first in Logseq's own lists), and all of the original's other features (two-way links, the tag map, search, and so on). In-place proposals, Option-Enter stepping, the Hyper-key tap and hiding empty properties were left out of the first version and are now ported.

## Other languages

Copy `en` in `core/dates.js`, change the weekday and month names, `relative` words, the patterns and `ahead`, register it in `main.js` (`Flow.locales`), and add a `locale` setting to `logseq/settings.js` (an enum of the two names, default `en`; with one language there is no row). `test/run.js` has a Spanish-weekday example. For languages that do not stem with a trailing s/ed/ing, set the engine's `stem` limit to `false` or give it a function (`core/engine.js`, `stemmer`).
